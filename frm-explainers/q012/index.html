THE QUESTION

An investor is assessing the market risk of Andromeda Fund. The fund has an expected annual return of 6.8% and volatility of 7.0% and is benchmarked against the S&P 500 Index. The investor assumes the S&P 500 Index has an expected annual return of 7.2% and volatility of 8.2%. According to the CAPM, if the risk-free rate is 2.2% per year, what is the beta of Andromeda Fund?

What is the beta of Andromeda Fund?

CORRECT ANSWER
A✓✕
0.92
Back out beta from the CAPM
CAPM
6.8% = 2.2% + β × (7.2% − 2.2%)
SOLVE FOR BETA
Excess return: 4.6%
Market premium: 5%
β = 4.6 ÷ 5 = 0.92
WHY NOT THE OTHERS?
B
0.95
2.2% + 0.95 × 5% = 6.95%, not 6.8%.
C
1.13
Beta above 1 implies beating the market — this fund earns less.
D
1.23
Also above 1 — can’t fit a fund earning less than the market.
KEY TAKEAWAY
1No correlation given? Back beta out of the CAPM
2β = excess return ÷ market premium
3Earns less than the market → β below 1

And the answer is...
A!
Here's why.
We don't know the correlation between
the fund and the index,
so we can't use the volatilities.
Instead, we back out beta from
the CAPM.
CAPM says the fund's return equals
the risk-free rate plus beta times
the market premium.
So 6.8 equals 2.2,
plus beta times 7.2 minus 2.2.
The fund's excess return is 4.6
percent.
The market premium is 5 percent.
Divide 4.6 by 5,
and beta is 0.92.
Now, why not the others?
B is wrong.
Plug in 0.95,
and the CAPM gives 2.2 plus
0.95 times 5,
which is 6.95 percent,
not 6.8.
C is wrong too.
A beta above 1 means the
fund should earn more than the
market.
But the fund's expected return,
6.8 percent, is below the market's
7.2.
And D?
Same problem.
It's also above 1,
so it can't fit a fund
that earns less than the market.
So, the key takeaway.
When correlation isn't given,
use the CAPM to back out
beta.
Beta equals the fund's excess return
divided by the market premium.
And if a fund earns less
than the market,
its beta should be below 1.
