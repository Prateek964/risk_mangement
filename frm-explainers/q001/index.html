THE QUESTION

A risk manager at a local bank is discussing expected and unexpected loss with a risk analyst. Unexpected losses can sometimes result from unknown or uncertain risks, or risks that are difficult to quantify. Which statement is correct for the manager to make?

Q1Which statement about unknown risks is correct?

CORRECT ANSWER

A risk manager’s confidence in the estimate of a risk measure should affect the application of that estimate in the decision-making process.

WHY NOT THE OTHERS?
A
Unknown risks may be estimated but are typically impossible to manage.
Unknown risks can still be managed — e.g. by avoiding the activity.
B
Unknown risks may exist in various risk types but are typically minor and inconsequential.
Knightian uncertainties can be huge in severity.
C
Risk managers should treat unknown risks in the same way as those risks that can be quantified.
Never treat an unmeasurable risk as a known quantity.
Knightian uncertainty
Risk where you can’t even model a probability distribution of outcomes.

A risk manager at a bank
is discussing expected and unexpected
loss.
The manager points out that unexpected
losses can come from unknown or
uncertain risks...
risks that are hard to quantify.
Which statement should the manager
make?
Option A.
Unknown risks may be estimated,
but are typically impossible to
manage.
Option B.
Unknown risks exist in various
risk types, but are typically minor
and inconsequential.
Option C.
Risk managers should treat unknown
risks the same way as risks
that can be quantified.
Option D.
A risk manager's confidence in
a risk estimate should affect how
that estimate is used in decision
making.
Pause the video and pick your
answer.
The correct answer is D.
Every risk number comes with a
level of confidence.
When you trust the estimate,
you can lean on it.
When you don't,
you add buffers,
stress tests, and judgment.
So your confidence in a risk
measure should shape how you use
it.
Now, why are the others wrong?
A is wrong.
Some unknown risks are Knightian
uncertainty...
where you can't even model a
probability distribution.
But they can still be managed,
for example by avoiding the activity
altogether.
B is wrong.
Knightian uncertainties can be
huge in severity,
even when their frequency is impossible
to measure.
C is wrong.
Never treat a risk you cannot
measure
as if it were a known
quantity.
Key takeaway.
Measure what you can,
respect what you can't,
and let your confidence guide your
decisions.
